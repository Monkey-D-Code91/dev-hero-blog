There's something that happens to me often, and maybe it happens to you too.

I listen to a song. I don't know anything about it yet. I don't know who wrote it, how it came to be, how many hands passed over it. All I know is that, at some point in the chorus, something inside me shifts by an inch. It happens just like that, without warning. I listen again. Then again. It ends up in a playlist I only open in certain moments, the ones where I need to feel exactly that thing.

Maybe it reminds me of someone. Or a summer that won't come back. Or simply the way I looked out the window one evening, coming home.

Then, one day, I find out that song was generated by artificial intelligence.

And I stop.

I don't know about you, but in that moment I feel the need to decide something. As if I had to take a stance. Do I delete it from the playlist? Do I leave it there, but with a different weight?

Before answering, though, I like to pause on a smaller, quieter question: did the song change, in that moment, or did I?

Because the song, in substance, stayed exactly the same. The same notes, in the same order, with the same breath between one phrase and the next. The only thing that moved was a piece of information that arrived *afterward*. And that information, on its own, seems to have the power to travel back in time and rewrite what I had felt.

It's strange, if you sit with it for a moment. Usually we let things speak to us, and we judge based on what they tell us. With music born from artificial intelligence, it's often the opposite: we listen to its origin first, and let that origin tell us whether the emotion we felt was allowed to exist.

Maybe it's because we assume that behind every emotion there has to be someone who suffered, or loved, or lost something, so that we can, in some way, receive it. But is that always true?

Many of the songs we carry inside us weren't even written by the people who sing them. There are authors who stay out of frame, by choice or by fate. Composers whose faces we'll never know. Producers who build an entire sonic world without ever setting foot on a stage. And yet, when a voice hits us and takes us apart, we rarely stop to ask who wrote that particular line. We simply let ourselves go.

So why should anything change with artificial intelligence?

Maybe it has to do with where we think music actually lives. Not in the recording studio. Not in the moment it's written, whether with a pen or an algorithm. It lives inside whoever is listening. Two people can sit next to each other, listen to the exact same track: one stays unmoved, the other is moved without quite knowing why. The song hasn't changed between one listen and the next. What changed is who was receiving it.

If that's true, if the emotion is born from that encounter too, and not only from whoever wrote the first note, then maybe it doesn't belong solely to the writer. It belongs, at least a little, to whoever stays to listen too.

There's a thought that always comes back, when we talk about time. We often hear about entire months spent in a studio, nights spent redoing the same chorus, as if that effort were already a guarantee of value. And when we find out a song was born in a few minutes, almost by chance, we feel it's more fragile, less deserving, as if the time spent were some kind of proof.

But when we look at a painting that leaves us breathless, we don't stop to count the hours it took to paint it. We judge what has reached us, not the path it took to get there.

It's a bit the same mechanism as an actor performing a monologue written by someone else. Those words aren't theirs. They didn't think them, didn't live them, maybe wouldn't even have chosen them. And yet, when they speak them on stage, something transforms. The voice, the breath, the silence before the next line: all of it becomes theirs, even though the thought never was. They simply gave it a form, and sometimes that's enough for something to reach us.

If a song written in ten minutes manages to stay with you longer than one built over six months in a studio, which of the two, for you, had more value?

That's not a question you need to answer right away. It's just a question worth leaving open, like a window on a warm evening.

I should also say one thing, for honesty's sake, before closing. Artificial intelligence doesn't come from nothing. It doesn't know what nostalgia is, has never lived through a love that ends, has no summer to think about when it gets dark. It learns from everything we, human beings, have written and sung and played for decades. In a sense, it remains a reflection of us. We're always the ones giving meaning to those notes, only this time we don't know for certain who arranged them in that order.

And maybe that, more than anything else, is what unsettles us. Not so much the machine itself. More the fact that we can no longer assume that behind every song there's someone with a story to tell us, sitting there, thinking of us as they write.

But if, in the end, we've always built that story too, every time we listen to something and it feels, for a moment, like recognizing ourselves in it, then maybe "who wrote it" isn't the question we actually need.

There's a song, somewhere in your playlist, that you've been listening to for years. You don't even remember why you saved it. And yet it's still there.

That song, whatever its origin, what has it left you with?
